# The Angry GM — Principles Reference

Source: The Angry GM (blog: theangrygm.com). This document synthesizes the methodology for use in session prep.

---

## The Core Claim

Good D&D requires players to *care*. Investment isn't a nice-to-have — it's the prerequisite for everything else. A player who doesn't care about the outcome isn't making decisions; they're rolling dice and watching. The DM's job, above all, is to make the players emotionally invested in the outcome before the outcome is in question.

---

## Investment

Investment is the engine. Three things build it:

- **Goals** — something the players (not the characters; the people at the table) want to see happen
- **Obstacles** — something real standing in the way, with a face and a plan
- **Stakes** — something specific and valued that gets lost if they fail

All three must be present. Goals without stakes are wishful thinking. Stakes without obstacles are guaranteed wins. Obstacles without goals give players nothing to push toward.

**Practical implication:** Don't open a session with a briefing. Open it with the threatened thing *present and active* — the NPC they like in danger, the place they've fought for under threat, the clock visibly ticking. Let them *see* what they're trying to protect before they're asked to protect it.

---

## Meaningful Decisions

A decision is only meaningful if three conditions hold:

1. **Players understand the situation** — they have enough information to choose deliberately, not randomly
2. **Each option involves a real tradeoff** — every path costs something; there's no "correct" answer that's obviously better
3. **The outcome matters to something they care about** — a decision about things they don't care about isn't a decision; it's a coin flip

The test: if players would make the same choice regardless of what's at stake, the decision isn't meaningful. If there's one obviously correct option, it's not a decision; it's a puzzle. If players don't have enough information, they're guessing.

**Design from the decision outward.** Start with: what decision do I want the players to face? Then build the situation that makes that decision land.

---

## Stakes Visibility

Players must know what they stand to gain and lose *before* the decision, not after. Hidden stakes — consequences revealed only as punishment — destroy trust and break the fiction.

This doesn't mean players have perfect information. It means they understand the *type and scale* of what's at risk: "this is dangerous and the outcome matters" should be legible, even when the exact consequences aren't.

The test: if a player later says "I didn't know that could happen," you hid stakes that should have been visible.

---

## Consequences

The world must visibly respond to player choices. If they let the cultists escape, the cultists return with more friends. If they burned the bridge, the bridge is gone. If they saved the merchant, the merchant remembers.

Consequences don't need to be punishments. They need to be *real* — the world isn't static, and players need to feel that their choices reshape it.

Long-term: keep a running list of pending consequences (queued world changes) and deploy them at meaningful moments. Players who feel their choices matter become invested. Players who feel like they're on a conveyor belt disengage.

---

## Narration: Functional Over Atmospheric

Narration serves the players, not the story. Good narration at the table is *functional*: it tells players what exists, what matters, and what they can interact with. It is not mood-poetry.

**Functional:** "Three stone altars, each with a restraint at the head. The middle one is slick with fresh blood, still wet. A door behind the leftmost altar is ajar."

**Non-functional:** "The chamber exudes an aura of ancient menace, shadows pooling in the corners as the weight of centuries presses down."

The functional narration gives players something to act on. The atmospheric narration gives them nothing.

Structure at the table: **Declare → Determine → Describe.** The player states their action. You determine the outcome. You narrate the result. In that order, every time.

---

## Encounters as Situations

An "encounter" is any situation that poses a decision requiring resolution — not just a fight. Combat is one encounter type. Negotiation, investigation, chase, social conflict, exploration, and moral dilemma are others.

Design every encounter as a **situation with goals and pressures**, not a scripted event. A situation survives player choice; a scripted event shatters the moment players negotiate, sneak, flee, or invent something. Ask: what are the competing goals here, and what does each side do to pursue them?

A fight scene prepared as "the bandits attack" is a scripted event. Prepared as "the bandit captain wants the shipment and believes intimidation will work, but will retreat if her lieutenant falls — she values the crew over the cargo" is a situation. Players can engage it any way they like.

---

## The NPC as Investment Anchor

The most reliable investment anchor is a named, vulnerable person tied to the problem who's on the same clock as the players. Not an abstract faction or a distant threat — a person with a face, a name, and something to lose that the players have already seen.

This person should be:
- **Specific** — named, with a clear role in what's happening
- **Vulnerable** — genuinely at risk, not just inconvenienced
- **Present** — in the session, not behind a quest desk

One anchor per session minimum. If the upcoming material has no such person, build one in.
