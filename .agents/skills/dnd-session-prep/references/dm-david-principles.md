# DM David — Principles Reference

Source: DM David (blog: dmdavid.com). This document synthesizes the methodology for use in session prep.

---

## The Core Claim

The best prep is targeted prep. Players will surprise you — always. The DM who over-preps a linear sequence of scenes wastes hours and runs them badly anyway, because the players went somewhere else. The DM who preps *information* and *situations* can run anything the players do, because the world is a state machine that responds to choices, not a track to ride.

---

## Secrets, Clues, and Leads (Not Scenes)

Don't prep scenes. Prep **information**.

- **Secret** — a truth in the world that's relevant this session. Something that exists whether or not the players discover it.
- **Clue** — a physical or observable thing that lets players discover the secret. Placed in the world; players find it by engaging the world.
- **Lead** — something that points players toward the next thing (NPC, location, clue chain). Players are never stranded if every node they reach contains a lead onward.

This structure survives any player approach. A prepped scene can be bypassed, skipped, or invalidated by player action. A prepped secret with three clues gets discovered no matter which angle the players take — because you placed the information in the environment, not in a sequence of events.

**Apply at every level:** at the session level (what truths are discoverable this session?), at the encounter level (what can players learn here?), and at the dungeon/location level (what does this room tell them?).

---

## The Three Clue Rule

Any conclusion the players *need* to reach to advance the story must have at least three independent paths to it.

One clue can be missed — they didn't search that room. Two clues can both be missed — bad rolls, distraction, wrong assumption. Three independent clues, delivered through different channels (NPC dialogue, physical evidence, observable consequence), are effectively impossible to all miss.

**"Independent"** matters: three clues all hidden behind the same skill check, or all in the same location, don't count as three. The paths must be distinct.

**Practical check:** for each secret in prep, list the three clues and ask "could they miss all three?" If yes, add a fourth.

## Prep Only What's Likely

Players will go somewhere you didn't expect. This is not a failure condition; it's the point. The mistake is wasting prep time on contingencies that won't materialize.

Prep discipline:
1. At the end of each session, note what the players said they'd do next (or your best inference)
2. Identify the two or three most likely directions
3. Prep those — and only those — in full
4. Have a rough awareness of adjacent possibilities, but don't detail them until they become likely

If players go somewhere unprepped, improv from the established facts of the world. You know the world; the players just went to a part of it you hadn't detailed. Use what you know and fill gaps with situational logic, not random improvisation.

## Delegation

You don't have to run everything at the table. The DM's cognitive load during a session is enormous; anything that can be offloaded should be.

Standard items to delegate to players:
- Initiative order tracking
- Condition and HP tracking (their own)
- Session journal (someone writes what happened)
- Map and miniature setup
- Rule lookups (let the player who wants to do something look up how)

When you offload logistics, you free yourself to focus on the fiction — what's happening in the world, what NPCs are doing, what comes next. That's the job. The rest is administration.

## Spotlight Management

Every player should have a moment each session where their character specifically matters — a decision that plays to their abilities, backstory, or choices. Left to chance, spotlight pools around the most assertive players and the most combat-focused characters.

Manage it deliberately:
- Before the session, earmark one spotlight moment per player
- These don't need to be big — a conversation with an NPC connected to their backstory, a skill check only they can handle, a callback to a decision they made
- Track who got spotlight at the end of the session; if someone consistently gets less, fix it next session

**Spotlight ≠ mandatory participation.** Some players prefer to support; spotlight for them might be a moment their support work pays off, not a solo scene.

## Pacing Levers

Sessions die in uncertainty. Players who don't know what to do next stall, argue, and lose momentum. The DM's job is to keep a clear choice visible at all times.

When pacing breaks down — investigation stalls, players debate without information, energy drops — reach for one of four levers:

1. **New information** — deliver a clue they haven't found yet; the world offers them something to act on
2. **A new threat** — something changes in the environment; inaction has a cost
3. **A ticking clock** — surface or advance a timer that was already in motion; urgency compresses decisions
4. **A consequence** — show the result of a prior inaction or choice; the world responds

The right lever depends on why pacing broke:
- Stuck on *what's true* → new information
- Stuck on *what to do* → a new threat or consequence
- Stuck in *deliberation* → a ticking clock

Never use all four at once. One clear signal is better than noise.

## Session-End Bookkeeping

At the end of every session, before you close anything:

1. Write down what the players said they'd do next (or your best inference)
2. Note what changed — timelines advanced, NPCs moved, resources spent, consequences triggered
3. Update `OPEN_THREADS.md` or equivalent with what's new, what's resolved, what's escalated

This bookkeeping is prep for the next session. Ten minutes now saves an hour of reconstruction later. The DM who doesn't do this spends the next session trying to remember where things stood instead of running them.

## The "No Gotcha" Principle

Player trust is fragile and essential. Don't use hidden information as a weapon:
- Risks should be visible before choices are made (players can see what they're walking into)
- Traps exist in the *world*, not in the DM's head (they're discoverable before they trigger)
- Monsters and NPCs behave logically (players can predict based on established behavior)
- Rulings are consistent (the same action gets the same treatment in similar circumstances)

A "gotcha" — a consequence that couldn't have been anticipated — breaks the fiction and breeds learned helplessness. Players stop engaging creatively when they believe the DM will punish clever solutions with arbitrary consequences.

Hidden mystery ≠ hidden gotcha. Players not knowing the answer is fine. Players having no way to figure it out is a gotcha.
